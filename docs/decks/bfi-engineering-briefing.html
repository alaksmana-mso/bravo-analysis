Summary/One page

Two origination platforms, one vendor core, and the same engineering habits on both sides

2loan origination platforms in production: Bravo (Java, Camunda) and LORA (Go, Temporal)
1vendor core loan system behind both: CONFINS, whose source we do not hold
~122production services on our GKE estate, in Go, Java and Node.js
Rp 636Ma month on three logging platforms, about 11× the tier the migration would retire

What we run and who runs it

Bravo is the older platform. It carries most of the loan book and most of the money, on a workflow engine whose community support ended in October 2025. Its teams are cut by loan step: scoring and underwriting, survey and verification, operations, contract and collateral. LORA is the re-architecture. It runs one product family in production, on Temporal Cloud, with three cross-domain teams that take a loan end to end. CONFINS is the vendor servicing core both platforms hand a loan to at go-live. The 2026 target architecture makes LORA the one orchestrator for every channel and product.

What is wrong, in one paragraph

Neither platform tests its orchestration layer. Both built a journey test suite and left it unable to run. Both watch for the wrong failure mode, so the failures they actually have never alert. Logging replaced tracing as the way engineers look at production, and it now costs more than the platform it observes. A security incident in the Bravo engine went unnoticed for three months. And the analysis that found all this was itself wrong several times and had to be corrected, which is a finding about how we measure.

The stacks differ. The habits do not. That is why moving to one platform will not, on its own, fix them.

Architecture/Today, as the Architecture team draws it

Today: two ways into origination, and loan data held in several places

Driver 1Application data is scattered

Onboarding Service, Bravo, LORA and Salesforce each keep their own copy. The fields differ. No one copy is accountable.

Driver 2Two origination paths

Onboarding into Bravo, and LORA, run side by side. Channels and products behave differently depending on the path.

Driver 3The core is moving

CONFINS R3 is replacing R1, product by product. We should not rebuild what R3 already gives us.

Driver 4New products need more

Non-retail products (PBF, SSF, corporate) need richer data and more flexible flows than today's design allows.

Driver 5Risk sits on its own

S1 and the PD model are separate pieces with nothing coordinating them. Adding a new risk signal is hard.

Architecture/The 2026 target

Target 2026: one orchestrator, one home for loan data, and a bigger risk system

One orchestrator. LORA takes every channel and every product. The Onboarding-into-Bravo path is retired.
A new Core Platform. Customer Service, the Application Data Platform (ADP), Product and Product Pricing become the single sources of truth. Changes stream to the warehouse through CDC (Debezium on Kafka).
Process steps get names. KYC, survey, e-contract, operation, e-signing and underwriting become separate steps that LORA calls. Today they live inside Bravo's engine.
Risk grows to four parts. A risk orchestrator, a decision engine that replaces S1 and the PD model, Verify One, and a new anti-fraud engine. Third-party data (Pefindo, Shopee score) feeds it directly. The Risk Department owns it.
Data. The warehouse gains a Customer Data Platform (CDP). Risk reads analytic data back from it.
Unchanged. The channels, CONFINS R1 / R3, asset management and shared capability. Loan Management is split into its sub-domains, each with a named building-block owner.

Architecture/Where the target meets what we measured

The direction is set. Five things we measured decide the pace and the order.

Most of the value movessequencing
Bravo carries most of the loan book and most of the money. LORA runs one product family in production: NDF, through the partnership door. Six more families exist in code but are not deployed. Three have no production deploy workflow, and no feature flag switches a family on. So “one orchestrator” means moving most of the money, not a small tail.
Bravo must stay safe for the whole moveurgent under any target
Bravo's workflow engine (Camunda 7 Community) lost support in October 2025. Spring Boot 3.5 lost it in June 2026. Bravo will run for the full migration window. A supported engine fork plus a Boot upgrade is 18 to 33 engineer-days, and it is needed whatever the target says.
LORA needs gates before more volumereadiness
Retries have no attempt limit. The planner's scheduling logic has no tests. The 150-scenario journey suite has nowhere to run, and the nightly fails the same 199 cases every night. These are cheap to build, but they have to exist before each product moves.
Named steps meet a design with no seamsteam shape
The target gives each process step its own owner. LORA keeps one document per loan with one planner, and its team says the design has no seam for a split by loan step. Its top production incident, 287 survey resets, crossed survey and task code. How steps are owned is the team-shape question on slides 11 and 12.
What the diagram does not showplatform
Observability, test gates and platform ownership. Today that is three log platforms at Rp 636M a month, 52 production services with no source we can read (mostly CONFINS and Treasury), and no named owner for LORA's shared SDK. Each of these sits under every box in the target.

Where our own analysis stands

The target was drawn in April 2026. Our analysis since then keeps the platform question open until three things are measured: the value split from a system of record, the Surveyor release rejects mapped, and the cost to launch one more LORA family. The Bravo engine fix does not wait for any of them.

Stack/LMS Core

CONFINS: the core we depend on, cannot read, and did not know we were paying to log

Both platforms hand a loan to CONFINS at go-live. It is a vendor product, it runs in its own GCP project, and it is the largest single source of production log volume we pay for.

What it is

AdIns CONFINS, written in C# on .NET. About twelve services: accounts receivable, foundation, LOS, amendment, payment, cash and bank, AR maintenance, government regulation, reconciliation, recovery, MoU. Plus its own Camunda external-task client and a family of batch workers. It runs in core-system-prod, a separate project and cluster from the Bravo estate. Bravo reaches it through a core proxy, an LMS gateway and the agreement service. The agreement service alone emits 7.4 million spans a week and sends zero logs to Datadog.

What is wrong with how we run it

52 of our 122 production services have no source code we can see, and CONFINS and Treasury are most of them. The service catalog owner field is empty. The deployment repository for it is not reachable to the people doing this analysis. Its logger writes every request and response body to disk: agreement numbers, customer numbers, outstanding balances, amortisation schedules. And because the pods write to a shared host path, the Datadog Agent re-reads a month of dead pods' files at every rollout. That one service was about a third of all indexed production log events. A first analysis called those lines "blank" because nobody opened the attributes.
~12CONFINS services in production, none with source in our repositories
~36Mlog events a week ingested from the CONFINS family, about 12M indexed
~90%of the largest service's volume is re-ingestion of dead pods' files, not traffic
1 dayof SRE work to fix the collection path, needing no vendor access

The way-of-working question here is ownership. A system both platforms depend on has no named owner, no readable source, and a logging bill nobody had attributed to it.

Teams/The two archetypes

Archetype 1 cuts teams by loan step. Archetype 2 cuts them by product, end to end.

Archetype 1 · Bravo Squads split by domain of the loan-application steps

Each squad owns one stage of the journey and the repositories for it. A loan crosses several squads on its way to go-live. The squad folders in our GitHub organisation, with repository counts:

Direct & Tele Marketing 21→ Digital Partnership 4→ Customer Platform 12→ Scoring & Underwriting 3→ Survey & Verification 4→ Operation Pre-Go Live 8→ Contract Collateral & Loan Calculation 10→ Operation Post-Go Live 9

Around them: Insurance 5, Payment 5, Agency 8, Asset Management 8, Supplier Platform 8, Digital Web 5, Customer Mobile Apps 2, and an Internal Service squad with 26 repositories. Two Jira projects carry Bravo LOS delivery: BLCS for Scoring & Underwriting and LN for Surveyor & Verificator. They share a sprint train and have zero cross-project issue links.

Archetype 2 · LORA Cross-domain teams that take a loan end to end

LORA is one squad organised as three sub-teams: LORA 1, LORA 2 and LORA 3. They work different features in parallel. Most tasks are executed end to end by one team, so members share a baseline understanding of the whole system. This is a team statement and the VMP LORA plan, not something we measured.

  • Why LORA went this way. A loan is one document with one planner. A "return to survey" change touches survey code and underwriting status writes at once. The architecture has no seam where a domain split would want one.
  • The stated condition for leaving a domain team. The departing owner first encodes their domain rules as business tests that guard the domain. Tests are the handover.
  • The natural boundary is the product family, not the loan stage. Seven families, each with its own document, workers and queue.
  • Supporting squads. LORA Core owns 8 shared repositories; LORA RL owns the revolving-loan family.

Neither shape is a property of its platform. Both are prior organisational decisions, and both can be changed.

Teams/What each shape produces

What we can actually see each archetype doing

Archetype 1 · observed
  • One change touches one or two repositories. That is the real content of "developing in Bravo is easier". Ticket counts do not support a speed claim in either direction.
  • A bus factor of one. In two 50-ticket samples, 18 of 20 long-lived Stories were assigned to a single account, on the service carrying most of origination. Sub-tasks spread; the shape of a change does not.
  • Cross-domain work becomes a hand-off. Underwriting return that must sync surveyor status appears as a ticket in one project with no link to the other.
  • Three consoles, three toolchains. The same design system at three versions, React 17 and 18, Node 18 and 20. A cutover gate that "runs the console suites" is three different jobs.
  • Backlogs are bulk-loaded. 45 of 50 tickets in one sample were created in a 37-minute window. Any created-to-resolved metric over that window measures a programme, not a cadence.
Archetype 2 · observed
  • One change touches about five repositories. Schema, SDK, worker, gateway, front end. Work is spread across 22 distinct assignees in 100 sampled tickets.
  • The team's own complaint: "one team must master all domains." The complaint is confirmed as the organisational cost of one document and one planner. The fix chosen is the end-to-end shape, not merging teams.
  • Nobody owns the platform yet. Four SDK versions, about 60 duplicated activity packages and a forked executor are the visible cost of every team being stream-aligned. The plan implies a platform team and does not name one.
  • The handover tests do not exist yet. 62% of automated activity packages have a test, the planner's readiness logic has none, four repositories have zero. You cannot exit into a net that is not built.
  • The top production incident spans both old domains. 287 survey resets from a survey and task-manager race. A domain-split team structurally could not fix its own top incident.

Where the analysis already landed

The LORA pack recommends Archetype 2 with a strict order: build the test net first, write the guard packs in pairs with product sign-off, name a platform team, pilot one product family, then roll out. It also names the early-warning signal: guard tests weakened without sign-off must stay at zero. This organisation has already silenced an inconvenient alert once rather than fix it, so that rule has to exist before the tests do.

Problems/Testing, both platforms

Testing: two large suites that cannot fail, and no test that runs a routing decision

Converges on bothNeither platform tests its orchestration layer

Bravo has 2,286 test files across four repositories. Four of them run a process. Its routing lives in BPMN expression strings, a database selector and a JSON matrix that no unit test can reach. LORA has 608 test files; its planner's scheduling logic, the thing that decides what runs, has no test. Neither CI can say which product a change hits.

Converges on bothBoth built a journey suite and left it unable to run

Bravo: 595 Cypress features, 46 authors, four workflows, frozen November 2023, the one scheduled job written so every step passes. LORA: lora-super-test, 150 scenario tests with real assertions, and a CI workflow with nowhere to run. The nightly Playwright run fails the same 199 API cases every night and the QA dashboard counts them as covered. Neither team knew the other had done the same thing.

Converges on bothGates that measure and cannot fail

Console coverage thresholds are 0%, 0% and 1%. Every SonarQube scan runs without waiting for its quality gate. A Codacy token is missing, so coverage upload fails on every PR. Neither platform sends CI events to Datadog, so nobody can say whether builds are getting slower or redder. The strongest gate in the estate is the consoles' unit tests, which do block a merge.

Bravo specific

Bravo cannot make an upstream fail on demand: its mock is one static, shared service. So 50 error handlers, 70 error definitions and 190 escalation paths have never been executed by a test, including a credit decision that bypasses anti-fraud after three failures. On the other side, its 113 typed clients mean the compiler catches stub drift for free, where LORA needs a script against a live schema registry.

The cheap part

The Bravo pack lays out a nine-layer ladder, L0 to L8, ordered by cost. Six layers do not exist. The two that would catch the most, model lint and gateway-expression evaluation, run in milliseconds and are about seven days of work. Five product-matrix journeys would cover the happy path of 100% of production volume; today zero do. This is a decision nobody made, not a mountain.

Problems/Logging and observability

Logging: Rp 636M a month to store what a trace would have answered

Rp 636Ma month: Cloud Logging 272, Coralogix 253, Datadog 111. A third of Cloud Logging is non-production.
93.6%of production log lines cannot be parsed. Lines over 16 KB are split by the container runtime.
~150×the contracted log ingestion. 256 GB a month was bought; about 1.3 TB a day is sent.
0log lines anywhere in production that can be joined to a trace.
Why teams log bodiesthe complaint is correct
Squads log full request and response bodies because Datadog cannot show them. They are right: no tracer setting puts an HTTP body on a span. The answer, Live Debugger, is blocked because Remote Configuration is failing on 13 production services with about 91,000 failed polls a week. So the workaround was rational, and asking teams to stop without a replacement will be resisted, correctly.
The wrappers made it the defaultfix once, not per service
The shared Java logging library turns body logging on by default and masks six keys by exact match. The Go wrapper's masking is a per-service deployment setting, and in production eight services set it to nothing. One Bravo service alone writes 487,000 bodies a week, 44% of its volume, unmasked.
What ended up in the logssecurity, not cost
Working chat webhook credentials in the URL of every message sent. A live API secret. Whole HR records about 900,000 times a week: religion, marital status, bank account, personal mobile. Customer phone numbers on every "not found" response. Each has an owner outside the logging programme and a clock on it.
Coverage gaps and split namestelemetry plumbing
Eleven busy services send no logs to Datadog at all. Eight are split across two service names, one for logs and one for traces, so they can never join. A family of monitors queries service names that do not exist and reports healthy forever. Six services emit neither logs nor traces.

The sequencing is the point. Clean the stream first, then enable better ingestion on a stream half the size. Enabling it today moves the waste from one invoice to another.

Problems/What cuts across all of it

Five habits that show up on both platforms and in the vendor estate

Habit 1Signals get silenced, not fixed

Bravo excluded its most informative engine error from its one business monitor. LORA excluded the go-live message from its activity-error alert and left loans wedged at attempt 26,707. The journey suite's steps all end in "pass anyway". Health monitors filter to 5xx only. In every case the signal was inconvenient and the fix was to stop looking.

Habit 2Instruments are on, nobody reads them

All four Bravo consoles and both LORA front ends had browser monitoring switched on for months. 2.4 million and 416,000 errors a week, respectively. Nobody had opened the dashboards. Camunda's engine logs carry activity names and versions; nobody ranked failures by them. LORA's APM spans carry the loan id; nobody grouped stuck loans by them.

Habit 3"Absence of one failure" means healthy

Bravo treats "not 5xx" as healthy and fails by 4xx and by parking a process. LORA treats "still running" as healthy and half its loans never finish. Both platforms' one business monitor is blind to the failure mode each actually has.

Habit 4Shared things have no owner

52 production services with no readable source. Feature flags in five mechanisms with no registry. A design system at three versions. Four SDK versions. Three log platforms and nobody can say what the second one is for. A catalog owner field that is empty for the core system. Every duplication we found sits where ownership stops.

Habit 5Gates that measure but cannot fail

Coverage at 0% thresholds. Quality gates never awaited. Scheduled jobs that cannot go red. A nightly that has failed the same cases for six weeks and counts as coverage. The estate is well instrumented for looking good and poorly instrumented for stopping a merge.

These are ways of working, not stack choices. Every one of them would survive a platform migration intact.
