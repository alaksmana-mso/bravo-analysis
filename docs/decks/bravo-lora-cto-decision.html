01/Executive summary

The brief in four lines

[ Problem ]

Bravo runs on two dead runtimes, and its engine has already executed foreign code.

Camunda 7 CE lost support on 14 Oct 2025; Spring Boot 3.5.x OSS on 30 Jun 2026. 61 injected process definitions are still loadable in production. One of them ran.

[ Architectural trade-off ]

The modelling paradigm is not the deciding variable. Engineering discipline is.

BPMN against GSM shaped roughly 6% of Bravo and 10% of LORA. The other ~90% looks the same on both. Bravo fails safely but invisibly; LORA fails visibly but never gives up.

[ Cost & visibility impact ]

Bravo is far cheaper and carries the money. It is also blind.

5–6.5× cheaper per application and ≈21× the business value — against zero traces, no loan id in logs and a frozen end-to-end suite. The fix is about 7 engineer-days, not a re-platform.

[ Recommended decision ]

Take A unconditionally. Defer B until two cheap measurements land.

A costs 18–33 engineer-days and buys a supported runtime. B is genuinely open: the evidence splits, and the two measurements that would close it cost days.

Sequence. Close the RCE exposure this week → fund the engine and Spring Boot upgrade this month → take gating measurements 1 and 2, which cost days → place the next product family. Only the last step is deferred, and only on evidence.

A does not wait for B. B does not block A. Bravo originates the retail book for at least another 12 months on every path in this deck. A Temporal rewrite is 12–18 months; a LORA migration 15–24. The Spring Boot upgrade is owed in all three.

02/[ Problem ]/Where we stand

Two origination platforms in production. Neither decision retires either one.

Bravo — the incumbent

  • Stack. Java 17, Spring Boot 3.5.16, an embedded Camunda 7.23 CE engine and PostgreSQL, all inside one service — ms-bpm — behind three operator consoles.
  • Model. The process is drawn, in BPMN, and executed by the engine. 53 BPMN files.
  • Load. 118,253 applications in August — 41% of volume, roughly 95% of business value. Four families live, a fifth in UAT.

LORA — the replacement

  • Stack. Go services on Temporal Cloud, spread over 13 repositories.
  • Model. Nothing is drawn. A custom planner (GSM) derives the next runnable step from the data a loan already holds.
  • Load. 171,479 applications in August — 59% of volume, ≈Rp 2.4bn of value. One family live out of seven built.

Migration state. The two run side by side and have since July 2026. Every path in this deck leaves Bravo originating the retail book for at least another 12 months. So neither decision is about finishing the migration. A keeps Bravo safe to operate. B places the next product family.

03/[ Problem ]/Runtime and exposure

Two runtimes past end of support. An engine that has already run someone else’s code.

 Verified in the code: pom.xml:26 pins 7.23.0. No Camunda BOM, no enterprise artifact, no licence key.

61 injected process definitions remain active in production

Textbook remote-code-execution attempts, uploaded across 7 sessions between 2026-05-23 and 2026-06-30. One executed and read back the hostname it ran on. All 61 are still startable.

The deploy path /engine-rest/** does require a credential, so this was a shared or leaked secret, not an open door. That secret — INTERNAL_SERVICE_KEY — grants full engine control. Separately, SecurityConfig.java:65 leaves /camunda/** on permitAll().

An unpatchable engine that still accepts uploads is an operational risk, not an architecture question. This is why Decision A is carved out and sequenced first. Nothing in the platform debate changes it, and nothing in the platform debate should delay it.

04/[ Problem ]/Scope control

What Decision B decides — and what it does not

B places the next product family for a 5–10 year horizon. Nothing else. Several arguments that circulate under B are already settled and should not be re-opened in the room.

In scope — genuinely open

  • Operability. Bravo’s workflow layer emits no tracing. Of 38 monitors, none watch a loan’s progress.
  • Testability. Bravo’s one scheduled end-to-end run cannot report a failure. Its suite has not changed since November 2023.
  • Compounding. Every family added to Bravo enlarges both gaps.
  • Hiring. Java, Spring and Camunda are commodity skills. Go plus an in-house planner is not. This is the one constraint headcount cannot move.

Out of scope — already settled

  • BPMN against GSM. Assessed on slide 06. Real, and not decisive.
  • Does Bravo work. Yes. 118,253 applications in August, with bounded failure and no zombie class.
  • Unit cost. Bravo, by 5–6.5× per application. Settled.
  • Accelerating Bravo’s retirement. Not recommended, on any reading of the evidence.
  • Team sizes. A lever you set, not a property of either platform.
  • The engine upgrade. That is A, and it already has an answer.

Judge a modelling paradigm by what teams ship under deadline pressure, not by what the spec permits. We accept the Bravo team’s review: the flowchart sprawl comes from how Bravo was modelled, and standard remedies exist. Of the three remedies, one is 5.5% rolled out, one is not started, and one is not enforced.

09/[ Cost & visibility impact ]/Observability debt

Bravo’s workflow layer is unobservable. That is an unfunded decision, not a platform law.

0Traces emitted by Bravo’s workflow layer
0Of 38 monitors that watch a loan’s progress
2023Last change to the end-to-end suite. The scheduled run cannot fail
~7 daysEngineering to close the highest-yield half of the gap

What is missing, precisely

No distributed tracing from the job executor. No application id in workflow logs, so a failure cannot be tied to a customer without manual correlation. No loan-progress monitors. The scheduled end-to-end run is wired so a failure cannot surface, and its 349 feature files are frozen at November 2023.

Operationally this means failures reach you as tickets from branches, not as alerts.

What closing it costs

The two test layers that catch the most — L0 and L1 — run in seconds and are about 7 engineer-days between them. The 349 existing feature files already cover the journeys; they need unfreezing, not rewriting. Add job-executor instrumentation and 4xx health gates and the observability half of the platform argument disappears.

Weeks, not quarters. And far cheaper than moving products to buy visibility.

The larger cost line is logging, not workflow

Logging runs at Rp 636M a month across three platforms — Cloud Logging Rp 271.8M, Coralogix Rp 253.3M, Datadog Rp 110.6M. That is about 11× the entire Rp 58M tier a Bravo migration would ever retire.

Rp 81–105M a month is recoverable from platform configuration alone — non-prod retention and exclusion filters, Cloud SQL audit logs off in non-prod, flow-log sampling. No code change, no squad sprint.

Visibility is the strongest argument against Bravo, and the cheapest to neutralise. Configuration money comes before migration money. The logging saving alone exceeds everything retiring Bravo’s workflow tier would deliver.

Decision A: swap the engine. 18–33 engineer-days, no licence.
