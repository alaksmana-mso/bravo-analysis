The problem/Verdicts

Six claims, checked against the data

The DF4W and DF2W boards show Bravo development moving faster

Nothing here to measure

DF holds 24 issues, 23 of them Epics. D2W holds 22, all Epics. Not one is assigned to anyone, and not one is resolved. These are product intake backlogs. You cannot time anything on them.

Bravo development is faster

Not supported

Bravo’s two delivery projects (BLCS and LN) created about 6,518 issues between January and August, against LORA’s 5,144 — so Bravo is ahead, 1.27×. That reverses our earlier 1.83× finding for LORA, which counted only BLCS. But a ticket count measures how finely work is split up as much as how much gets done, so this retires the measure rather than supporting the claim. In August the median time from created to resolved is 2 days on both. In June LORA was faster.

…because the team now knows what to do

Tenure explains it just as well

Bravo’s team has worked on this codebase since 2022 — 94 authors all-time, 23 of them active in 2026. That is long tenure on a four-year-old codebase. It is not a platform being easier to learn.

Developing in Bravo is easier

Supported

One change produces fewer pieces of work. A Bravo ticket splits into [BE], [FE] and [QA]. The same change in LORA splits into five repositories and five deploys. That is real, it is measurable, and it is the defensible core of the claim.

Bravo is easier to join

Half true

True on skills: Java, Spring and Camunda are common and easy to hire for. Not true on the codebase: a new hire has to read a 498,000-line monolith in which the product is identified in five different places. The advantage is used up by week three.

DF2W is a live Bravo product delivering faster than LORA

Refuted

Corrected 2026-09-10: DF2W is not live at all. It is in UAT, with a penetration test running against the LOS, and its go-live epics are still open. This pack had called it “fully configured in production”, which reads as released but unused. It is pre-release. It has started zero applications in 90 days. DF4W carries 5.5% of Bravo’s volume. The two products the claim cites are the smallest things Bravo runs — and one of them is not running at all.

Jira bfifinance.atlassian.net, all three cited boards listed in full · bravo-people.md §Verdicts02

The through-line

The claim is about what it is like to work in a platform, and it is being defended with how fast tickets close. Ticket speed cannot settle it. The measure reversed direction the moment we added a project we had missed, which is the clearest possible sign it was never measuring what it was asked to measure. On what it is like to work in, the claim is right — and until now nobody had measured how big the effect is: five repositories per change, against one or two.

What the data will not support

No throughput measure here can be trusted, in either direction. The ticket ratio reversed when we added a missing Bravo project: LORA ahead 1.83× became Bravo ahead 1.27×. A measure that swings that far on a scope fix is not measuring delivery. On cycle time, the August medians tie at 1 to 2 days across all three projects. June’s apparent gap in Bravo’s favour comes from batching releases and loading a backlog, not from architecture.

What the data confirms

One integration change touches 1 or 2 repositories and takes 1 or 2 deploys in Bravo — two when a console is involved. The same change in LORA touches 5 repositories and takes 5 deploys, in a required order. That is the whole real effect, and it survives the scope correction: two is still not five.

§3/What we found

Ticket volume: once LN is counted, LORA’s lead reverses

Bravo · BLCSBravo · LN (added 2026-09-10)LORA · BL

Corrected 2026-09-10. With Bravo’s second delivery project LN counted — 3,705 issues, more than BLCS — the January to August totals are Bravo 6,518 and LORA 5,144. That is Bravo ahead 1.27×, and 1.81× in July and August. What this slide said before — LORA ahead 1.83×, its lead narrowing from 3.2× to 1.5× — came from counting one of Bravo’s two projects against all of LORA’s.

Method

Jira hands out issue numbers in order within a project, so the first number after a month boundary tells you how many were created before it. We read the boundaries directly: BLCS-1939, LN-2843 and BL-4795 at 1 January, through BLCS-4752, LN-6548 and BL-9939 at 1 September. We could only read LN at four boundaries, so we pooled the windows two months at a time, and pooled the other two projects the same way.

We tested for double-counting on 2026-09-10. The two projects do not overlap.

If a single change were a BLCS back-end story and an LN console story, adding the projects together would count it twice. Four checks say that does not happen. (1) BLCS is Team Scoring & Underwriting and LN is Team Surveyor & Verificator — they are split by squad and business area, not by tier. (2) LN sub-tasks are 18 [BE], 15 [FE] and 7 [QA] — full-stack work, not the front-end half of something. (3) The capability prefixes do not overlap: [DF2W Scoring] and [UW 4W] against [DF2W] [O] and [DF2W] [S]. (4) There are zero links between issues in the two projects. Of 80 sampled summaries, no pair describes the same change. So the range collapses to its top end: 6,518, or Bravo ahead 1.27×. What inflation is left comes from per-sprint QA tickets written once per squad — a handful, not a doubling. The number gaps remain an upper bound for all three projects, since D2W has 14 of its 36 numbers unused.

What this does not mean — and the caution now cuts Bravo’s way

It does not mean Bravo ships 1.27 times as much product change. A ticket count measures how finely work is split up at least as much as how much gets done. We used that argument to discount LORA’s apparent lead. We now have to use it to discount Bravo’s. The right conclusion is that ticket volume cannot settle this either way. See §6.

1 · In August the medians are 1 to 2 days everywhere

All three projects are indistinguishable — LN 1 day, BLCS 2 days, BL 2 days. And LN, Bravo’s larger project, has both the lowest median and the lowest mean. Whatever difficulty LORA imposes does not show up here. Adding Bravo’s missing project does not open a gap in Bravo’s favour either.

2 · Both June figures are backlog loads, not working rhythms

LN’s 68 days comes from 45 of its 50 issues being created in a 37-minute window on 2026-06-01, between 21:19 and 21:56 — the [DF2W] [O] and [S] stories. That interval measures how long the DF2W programme took, not how fast tickets turn around. The 5 issues in that sample that were created normally have a median of 0 days. BLCS’s 9 days is the same effect: nine Stories created on 2026-06-01 and closed together on release. Both Bravo projects loaded DF2W backlog that day — it is the programme kickoff, seen twice.

3 · The same two-humped shape on both

A mass of sub-tasks closing in under three days, and a tail of Stories that close on release. That is one delivery process running on two platforms, which is exactly what §2 predicted.

Limits. Each sample covers one to six working days. It is a snapshot of two release cycles, not a distribution over the year. The windows differ in length because the projects create tickets at different rates, and LN is the fastest of the three. The June LN sample is a backlog load and is not a working rhythm at all; the August windows are the ones to compare. Every sample leaves out unresolved issues, which cuts the tail short equally on all of them.

§5/Withdrawn 2026-09-11

Team size is a decision already taken, not a property of the platform

This section used to make an argument. We are withdrawing it.

Earlier versions read “Seven engineers is the whole Bravo LOS delivery capacity”, and called it the strongest argument against putting new product families on Bravo. That is wrong. Bravo’s headcount is the result of an earlier CTO decision to consolidate engineering onto LORA — not a property of Bravo, BPMN or Camunda. The office that decides where a new product family goes also sets the headcount, and can change it. Using the consequence of a decision as evidence about that decision is circular.

The measurement still stands. It is the conclusion drawn from it that does not.

The number was wrong as well as circular

The 7 counted BLCS only. Sampling LN the same way finds 10 more people, none of them the same, and git history across Bravo’s four LOS repositories over 12 months shows 25 people. The figure used against Bravo was too low by a factor of three — which makes withdrawing it easier, not harder.

And it takes §3 down with it

§3 has now been overtaken twice. It explained LORA’s 1.83× lead as work being split more finely; headcount was a second explanation pointing the same way. Then the 1.83× itself turned out to be an artefact of the missing LN project, leaving Bravo ahead at 1.27×. The instruction is unchanged and now applies in Bravo’s favour: no per-team speed claim built on this measures the platform. §6 survives, because it counts pieces of work per change rather than tickets per team.

What survives — and it is now Bravo-specific

In both samples, almost every Story is assigned to a single account, while sub-tasks spread across everyone else — 9 of 9 long-lived Stories in June, 9 of 11 in August. Whoever holds the shape of a Bravo change today is a bus factor of one: if that person is unavailable, the work stops — on the service carrying about 94% of origination. That is a live operational risk needing an owner whatever is decided about new products. Answered 2026-09-11: LORA’s wider spread is deliberate. Per the VMP LORA plan it runs as three sub-teams (LORA 1, 2 and 3) working different features in parallel, and because most tasks are done end to end, members share a baseline understanding of the whole system. So this no longer argues for neither platform: concentration is a Bravo-specific risk, and LORA has a structural answer Bravo does not. It is a team statement about how LORA is organised, not a measurement in this pack.

And one row gains weight: who you can hire

If capacity is going to be added, the question becomes which platform can BFI actually hire for? Java 17, Spring Boot and Camunda 7 are common skills. Go plus a bespoke GSM planner plus Temporal is not — LORA’s own people.md puts it as “you can get Java or Golang developers, but they won’t be able to immediately read the code.”

This is the one staffing-related fact the CTO office cannot reverse: it is a property of the labour market, not of a headcount decision — and it favours Bravo. §7 bounds it: the advantage comes all at the start and is largely used up by week three.

“The team now knows what to do” is still explained by how long they have been there, not by how many of them there are. Bravo’s assignees have been on this codebase for up to four years. LORA’s include people who joined this year and are closing tickets at the same median. Knowing what to do is a property of people and their tenure, and people can be moved. It is not a property of either platform.

This is the whole defensible content of “faster and easier”, and it is worth taking seriously. A monolith with one relational data model really is fewer moving parts per change than thirteen repositories with a schema-first ordering rule. It also explains §3 without appealing to productivity at all: LORA writes roughly 2 to 5 times as many tickets per change as Bravo, so once you divide that out, the two teams may well be shipping at similar rates with very different bookkeeping. This argument matters more now that LN is counted, not less. Raw ticket volume now favours Bravo 1.27×, and the same correction that discounted LORA’s old 1.83× lead discounts Bravo’s new one.

What the five-repository cost buys

Products stay isolated at the data layer, contracts are checked against a schema, and you can add an automated step with no change to the orchestration at all — 172 activities against 3 hard precursors.

What the one-repository change pays

One job executor, one deployment, and a BPMN edit that ships for every product at once.

Neither is free. The claim is right that Bravo’s cost per change is lower. It says nothing about what that cost buys.

§8/What we found

What the claim gets right, and what it does not

“Developing in Lora is hard”

Partly true

And true in a specific way: five repositories, a required order to work in, schema changes that may only add, and eight worker versions running at once. Measured in §6.

“Developing in Bravo is faster”

Not supported

The ticket measure reversed on a scope correction — LORA ahead 1.83× became Bravo ahead 1.27× — which retires it in both directions. What is left is an identical August median and a slower June median. No reliable throughput measure favours either platform.

“…and easier”

Supported

One repository, one deploy, and no ordering across services. This is the real effect, and it is worth about 3 to 5 fewer pieces of work per change.

“…because the team now knows what to do”

Tenure explains it

Bravo’s assignees have been on this codebase for up to four years. LORA’s include people who joined this year, closing tickets at the same median. Knowing what to do is a property of the people, not of the platform — and people can be moved. Headcount is not evidence here: see §5, where that argument is withdrawn.

A fifth thing the claim does not say, and should

The two products it cites are the two smallest things Bravo runs. DF4W is the only product on the unified spine, and it takes 5.5% of applications started over 90 days. DF2W is not live yet — it is in UAT with a penetration test running — and has taken zero. If those are the reference implementations for “Bravo is easier”, then that easiness has not yet been tested at volume, on the monoliths, where about 94% of the book actually runs and where 2026 saw more commits than the spine did.
