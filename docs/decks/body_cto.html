04/Decision B

What Decision B is — and what it is not

You are deciding this

  • Observability. Bravo emits zero spans for its entire orchestration layer and has zero process-level monitors among 38.
  • Testability. Bravo’s only scheduled journey run cannot report a failure, and the suite behind it has been frozen since November 2023.
  • Whether adding surface to an unwatched platform is acceptable — each new product family adds more of it.
  • Which platform BFI can actually hire for, if capacity is added. Java/Spring/Camunda is a commodity skill set; Go plus a bespoke GSM planner is not. This is the one staffing-adjacent fact a CTO cannot reverse by reallocating people — and it favours Bravo.

You are not deciding this

  • BPMN versus GSM. Assessed in full — and the next two slides show the work rather than asking you to take it on trust: five rationale claims tested against the code, what each paradigm did better, and the Bravo team’s review. The paradigm shaped ~6% of Bravo’s codebase and ~10% of LORA’s, and favoured LORA there; the other ~90% turns on engineering discipline. Real, settled, and not load-bearing for this decision.
  • Whether Bravo works. It does. 76,446 applications in August, bounded failure, no zombie-loan class.
  • Whether to retire Bravo faster. Explicitly not recommended.
  • Which platform is cheaper. Bravo is, by 4–5× per application. Settled.
  • How many engineers each platform has. That is your lever, not a property of either platform — it is the output of the previous decision to consolidate on LORA, and it has been removed from the evidence in this deck.
  • Whether Bravo’s engine gets fixed. That is Decision A, and it is already answered.

A paradigm is fairly judged by what teams build with it under delivery pressure — not by what it permits in principle. 05/Decision B — the paradigm question, assessed

Why it is not the load-bearing argument

The verdict on the rationale The paradigm decided the shape of ~6% of Bravo’s codebase and ~10% of LORA’s — the automated pipeline — and decided it in LORA’s favour. The other ~90% looks structurally similar in both, and the differences there come from engineering discipline, not GSM versus BPMN.

Bravo (pure workflow) did better at

  • Bounded, classified failure — no zombie-loan class by construction. A claim about the shape of failure, not its frequency
  • A designed dead-letter path — the “visible failure” LORA’s own reliability doc asks for
  • Fleet queries — “all loans stuck at survey” is a WHERE clause
  • A forced retry policy — an engine that makes you write one ends up with a better one than an engine that lets you skip it
  • Orchestration-tier cost — ≈Rp58M/month against ≈Rp431M

LORA (GSM + Temporal) did better at

  • Adding an automated step — 172 activities, 3 hard precursors, no orchestration edit. The clearest validated win
  • One artefact — the whole loan in one schema-validated document with a version chain
  • Parallelism for free — ReadSet/WriteSet locking against Bravo’s 5 parallel gateways in 53 files
  • Product isolation at the data layer — separate documents and queues; Bravo has one job executor for everything
  • Observability of the pipeline — a span per activity attempt; Bravo has none

The Bravo team reviewed this, and their review was accepted

Four responses were checked against the code (§8). None moved the verdict, two sharpened it, and the fourth reversed a cost claim in Bravo’s favour — like-for-like, Bravo’s orchestration tier is the cheaper one. Their central point is correct and accepted: the flowchart explosion and the sequential flows are consequences of how Bravo was modelled, not laws of BPMN, and the standard remedies exist. It does not change the finding, because those remedies are respectively 5.5% deployed, not started, and not enforced — and a paradigm is fairly judged by what teams build with it under delivery pressure, which is the only condition under which either will ever run.

06/Decision B — the correction that matters most

Applications were the wrong unit, by a factor of about 47

Every rate in this analysis divides by an application count from a billing sheet. On that measure LORA carries 69% and Bravo 31%, and the previous version of this brief used it as a reason to prefer LORA. Measured by business value the ratio inverts.

LORA is live on one product family, not seven

The prod cluster runs only the NDF workers. The live set is NDF2W (90.2% of loans) and NDF4W (9.8%), both through the partnership door. Six of seven families are un-deployed — three have no production deploy workflow at all, and three more have a copied one naming a schema version that does not exist. There is no switch to flip: enabling a family is a deploy-and-launch project.

So the previous claim — “already carries 69% of applications across seven product families” — was wrong. That 69% all runs on the one family.

This pack applied its own criticism asymmetrically — then over-corrected

Our analysis faulted Bravo for DF2W being “fully configured, zero applications” while crediting LORA for six families in that state. That double standard was real. But the two are not the same state: DF2W is in UAT with a live pen test and a dated go-live path; LORA’s six await a decision and three cannot deploy at all. On this row the edge is Bravo’s. What survives: unlaunched families tell you nothing about ease of building, on either platform.

What is missing entirely

No agreement, disbursement, NTF or revenue data exists anywhere in either platform’s records. The billing sheet has 14 cost columns and 2 application-count columns. The Rp figures above are a CTO-office estimate, period and basis to be confirmed — and since the ~47× ratio is load-bearing, confirming it is gating measurement #1.

05/The counter-case, taken seriously

Bravo’s strongest argument is cost — and it erodes without anyone acting

This is the one place the evidence favours Bravo decisively, and it should not be minimised: ≈Rp58M against ≈Rp431M for the orchestration tier, and 4–5× cheaper per application. One deployment, one database, against fixed contracts and over-provisioned capacity.

Why the gap is not the paradigm

LORA’s cost is ~86% fixed and its levers are time-locked, not architectural: a 3-year CPU commitment, a Temporal commitment renegotiable at ~March 2027, 448 GB of requests running at 15.5% utilisation, and five idle worker versions. Provisioning, not physics.

Why it erodes on its own

Bravo’s platform cost is fixed and its volume is falling by design — −35% in one month. At 40k applications the same ≈Rp58M is ≈Rp1,450 each and the gap halves with nobody doing anything. Meanwhile LORA absorbed +23% volume for +3% spend: the next product is close to free to host.

And the biggest cost lever is unrelated to the decision

Cloud Logging alone is Rp140.5M/month — 2.4× the entire tier the migration would retire — and it is a configuration fix behind a global Feign setting. A team that fixed logging config would save more in September than retiring Bravo LOS will save when the migration completes. Do it either way.

09/Decision B

Observability and testability — the two that are real, and both are cheap to fix

Observability — the process is invisible

Zero Camunda spans. Seven operation names cover every span the service emits and none of them is a process, a service task or a job execution — because all 483 service tasks run on job-executor threads that no HTTP request started. The 90% of Bravo’s work that is the loan moving through the flow produces no trace at all.

No log line carries the application id, so a failure cannot be joined to a customer without leaving Datadog. 38 monitors, zero on the process; 8 permanently red, 6 that cannot fire. A health model of “not 5xx” missed 748,686 404s a week reaching 69% of surveyor sessions.

Testability — the proof was switched off

Bravo wrote 595 Cypress journey tests across 46 authors and aimed 349 of them at the Surveyor Platform — the console generating 28.4% of its tickets. Then it froze the repository in November 2023 and left the one scheduled workflow with every step ending || true, so it can never report a failure.

An absent test cannot mislead anyone. A permanently green one can. And no Bravo test can make an upstream fail, so 50 error handlers, 70 error definitions and 190 escalation paths have never been executed — including an anti-fraud bypass that is a credit-policy decision reached by an exception handler.

One point in Bravo’s favour: the corpus’s 130 API feature files assert on 87% of their Then steps — competent Gherkin, and the best-written in the estate. The defect is confined to the 465 UI files, where 57% of steps are pure clicking and typing. The skill is present; it was not applied where it mattered and then it was switched off.

Each new product family adds surface, and on this platform nothing watches it. Capacity used to be the third panel here. It has been removed: headcount is the output of the previous decision to consolidate on LORA, so citing it to the office that holds that lever is circular. What is left is a statement about engineering discipline — and it is fixable in days-to-weeks, which is why it is a reason to fund remediation rather than to move products.

11/Decision B — symmetric risk

What you are accepting, whichever way you decide

If the next product goes to Bravo

  • You are committing to a community fork of a dead engine. Decision A lands Bravo on Operaton or CIB seven — maintained, but on a 6-month support line or an unpublished paid contract. Today’s exposure is drift; that would be a chosen dependency, and it attracts different scrutiny.
  • The new product will be as unobservable as the existing ones. Zero orchestration spans, no application id in logs, no process-level alert — so its failures surface as support tickets.
  • It inherits five product-discrimination mechanisms. Adding a product touches all five, and no test or CI artefact maps a change to the products it affects.
  • It inherits the in-flight deploy hazard. A shared sub-process redeploy reaches loans already running — today that is 73% of volume with no migration plan.
  • You get a genuinely cheaper tier and the lowest per-change cost in the estate. That is the trade.

If the next product goes to LORA

  • You pay a five-repository tax per integration change — schema, handler, activity, authorisation, front end, in a mandated order. Real, measured, and fixable with a generator that is designed but not built.
  • You inherit uncapped retry and a class of loans — roughly half — that never reach a terminal state while every signal reads healthy. LORA’s reliability edge is partly an artefact of failures nobody can see.
  • You cannot ask fleet questions about business state. “All loans stuck in survey” has no answer in LORA at all.
  • You cannot tell which upstream is degrading until a merged PR is actually released.
  • You are betting on a platform proven on one product family. Six of seven are un-deployed, three without a production deploy workflow — so “LORA hosts seven families” is a statement about repositories, not production.
  • You cannot yet show that LORA converts. No agreement, disbursement or revenue data exists for it. On the CTO office’s figures it earns ≈Rp 14,000 per application against Bravo’s ≈Rp 657,000.
  • You get near-zero marginal cost, product isolation by construction, version-pinned deploys, and a journey test that asserts. That is the trade.

Both columns are now roughly the same size, which is the honest position: LORA’s risks are unproven scale, Bravo’s are unfixed discipline, and both are addressable with engineering that has already been scoped. That is why Decision B is presented as open rather than answered.

14/The security half of Decision A

The finding that makes Decision A urgent rather than tidy

Camunda RCE executed on production BPM via an authenticated deploy path

The production ms-bpm Camunda engine contains 61 process definitions that are not part of the application, deployed across 7 distinct sessions between 2026-05-23 and 2026-06-30. They are textbook expression-injection remote-code-execution probes — reflection chains that build java.lang.Runtime through string concat() to dodge keyword filters, then run OS commands and capture stdout into a process variable.

All 61 are still active and startable today, and there is evidence in the engine’s own history that at least one executed. Camunda Cockpit is permitAll() at the Spring Security layer — but corrected 2026-09-10, that is not the vector. The deploy path is /engine-rest/**, which requires a Keycloak token or the shared api-secret; the shared secret grants full engine rights, and no ProcessEngineAuthenticationFilter is registered, so Camunda’s own authorization never applies. Someone held a valid credential.

This was found incidentally while querying the production database for the workflow analysis. If it was an authorised penetration test, treat this as confirmation the artefacts are still in production. If it was not, this is an incident.

Why it is here rather than in the scoreboard
